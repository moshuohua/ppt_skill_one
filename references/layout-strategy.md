# Dynamic layout strategy

Choose the layout from the claim and relationship. Never begin from a fixed left-chart/right-text template.

## Selection sequence

1. State the one conclusion the reader must retain.
2. Identify the relationship that proves it.
3. Choose the visual form that exposes that relationship fastest.
4. Give the visual the dominant area and place evidence labels next to what they explain.
5. End the reading path at the business implication.

## Useful consulting-style families

| Layout family | Use when | Typical silhouette |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chart dominant | One quantitative comparison or trend proves the point | Large chart with a compact implication rail |
| Control-point migration | Competitive power, profit, or ownership is moving | Before-after, diagonal ascent, or value-chain shift |
| Causal or evidence chain | Several linked facts lead to one conclusion | Wide chain, driver tree, or converging evidence |
| Architecture stack | The point depends on layers, boundaries, or ownership | Vertical stack with highlighted control layer |
| Comparison matrix | A small number of dimensions distinguish options | Flat matrix with one decisive pattern highlighted |
| Evidence to action | External evidence must translate into a company response | Evidence field flowing into one action block |
| Central thesis | Several independent supports converge on one judgment | Central claim with two or three restrained supports |
| Sequence or timeline | Order, maturity, or migration path matters | Horizontal or vertical path with a clear endpoint |

## Composition rules

- Use one composition and one visual focus.
- Let evidence sit beside the node, point, or transition it supports.
- Vary scale and whitespace to create hierarchy; do not package every idea into an equal card.
- Avoid dashboard grids, repeated rounded cards, decorative icon rows, and excessive dividers.
- Keep no more than five nodes in one horizontal sequence. Turn a longer chain into layers or a vertical path.
- Prefer native charts, shapes, text, and connectors. Use a raster illustration only when it explains information that native objects cannot.
- Keep the business implication visually connected to the evidence rather than isolated as a generic footer.

## Relationship-to-layout test

Before building, complete this sentence:

> The reader should see **relationship X** in the main visual, which proves **claim Y** and leads to **action Z**.

If the chosen layout does not make X visible without reading every sentence, choose another layout.
